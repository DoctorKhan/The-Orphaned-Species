# Book I Chapter 2 — Intake Corridor Seed

> **Status: SEED — not prose draft.** This is a one-paragraph prompt showing Eli’s intake corridor as a metrology ritual, so the system appears on-page before it becomes a concept. The paper, palm/face terminal, wage ticket, and biological scan are not bureaucratic color — they are the secular surface of an older civic measurement inherited from keeper lineages the Mandate now operates without remembering.

---

The corridor is long enough to strip the island off you. Fluorescent panels overhead, floor scuffed to the grey of repeated passage. Wren has rehearsed the intake story twice: contract labor, Stack maintenance shift, legitimate manifest. She says it to the officer in the booth the way she would say a prayer — not because she believes it will be answered, but because the shape of the words has always been what got people through.

Papers first. Wren lays Eli’s papers on the counter. The officer turns the pages without reading them — the Stack does not read, it verifies. Each page is a measurement: wage ticket stamped, sponsor line filled, biometric run already checked against the ledger before they reach the booth. Eli’s name is not on the ledger. That is the risk Wren is managing with the rehearsed story and the green cord knotted in her pocket.

Palm and face terminals next. The officer holds out the reader. Eli places his palm on the glass. The machine takes print and heat and microvascular rhythm in the same pass. Then the face terminal: eyes on the lens, hold still. The camera is reading the same face the wage ticket names, the same face the biometric thread confirms, the same face the biological scan will classify. The Stack does not need to know who Eli is. It needs to know whether the face, the ticket, the print, the scan, and the ledger agree — whether the person standing in the corridor compresses into the same ordered record twice.

The wage ticket is thin cardstock, printed at the Reclamation before the boat left. It says contract labor, Stack maintenance shift, days eight to fourteen, supervisor Suresh bin Ali, wage class four. The ticket is the bridge between Eli’s body and the Stack’s account: the body produces labor value, the ticket names the rate, the rate enters the perimeter ledger, the ledger decides whether the body is worth the heat it releases. A body that does not reduce to a ticket is unclassified, unsponsored, Extra.

The biological scan is the corridor’s real instrument. It is not looking for disease. It is measuring the exergy Eli’s living system destroys by existing inside the Stack’s perimeter — the ordered gradient his metabolism consumes and cannot restore. Sleep debt from the crossing shows as a thermal signature. The receiver’s latent charge shows as an autonomic spike the machine has not been trained to read. The officer does not see the spike. The machine does not name it. But the data leaves the corridor in a packet the Mandate’s back systems will compare against carrier registries kept from the outbreak years.

Nothing in the corridor is new. The papers, the palm and face terminals, the wage ticket, the scan — they are the same instruments the teahouses and stone circles once used to measure readiness for a charged-ground exposure, consent-bound and answerable to the measured. The Mandate kept the instruments. It lost the consent.

The scan pings. The officer looks up for the first time.

“Secondary assessment,” he says.

The words are soft. The mechanism is not.
