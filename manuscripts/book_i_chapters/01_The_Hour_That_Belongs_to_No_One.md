# The Orphaned Species — Book I: The Breach
## Chapter 1 — The Hour That Belongs to No One

### I

The race begins before the sun clears the hill.

Eli stands in the hull of the skiff with his weight forward, left hand on the mainsheet, right on the tiller. The hull is a thing of woven bamboo and scavenged resin, light enough for two men to carry, shaped to plane across the short fetch between Pelangi Reach and the Meridian breakwater. Rasel built it. Eli has painted the name *Garden* on the transom in white enamel, letters slightly uneven because he was twelve and the hull was already old when he did it.

Kiran’s boat is called *Kiran*. It sits at the start line with a new-looking spinnaker and a hull that smells of factory resin rather than stored bamboo. Kiran does not talk to Eli before the start. He talks to Suresh, who is the race marshal and also the person holding the wager.

“You’re going to lose on the downwind leg,” Kiran says.

Suresh does not answer. He holds up a hand. The wind is already moving through the rigging.

The Reach is still dark. The water is dark. The hill behind the village is dark except for the three lights that mark the shrine path and the blue navigation lamp at the end of Babaji’s pier. Eli knows those lights the way he knows his own hands. He was raised in this water. His father taught him to read the chop off the headland before he could read print. His mother taught him to sleep on a moving boat without fear.

He does not think about them when the start horn blows. He thinks about the wind.

*Garden* lifts and runs. The first hundred meters are all balance — find the groove, listen to the hull, let the boat tell you where it wants to go before you make it go there. Eli feels it the way he feels a patient’s pulse before he touches the wrist: the body of the boat is already asking for a correction he has not given yet.

Kiran is ahead by a boat length at the mark.

The wind shifts. Eli gybes early, takes the wider arc, and lets *Garden* surf down the back of the wave while Kiran’s new hull pounds through the chop. By the time they round the final buoy, *Garden* is half a length ahead. Eli does not look at Kiran. He looks at the shore, where Suresh is already raising his hand.

He wins by a skiff length.

The wager is the Cherry Cube weekend manifest: cash, a worker pass for the Stack intake corridor, and Shireen’s clinic wire. Suresh has held it for three weeks because Eli needed to clear his work record and prove he could handle a Stack shift without drawing attention. The Cube is a choreographed idol show in Meridian City’s underground mall circuit — three days of rehearsals and backstage labor that earns enough to move a small group north. Eli has been planning this escape since before he knew he needed to escape.

He ties *Garden* to the public rack at the quay. His hands are shaking. He tells himself it is the cold.

---

### II

Mei cooks mee goreng on a single burner on the terrace above the quay. She has set out four plates because she expected both racers, but Kiran leaves after the race without eating. He does not speak to Eli. He does not look at him. He leaves the way people leave when they have already decided they will not stay.

So it is just the two of them: Mei and Eli, sitting on plastic chairs with their plates on a folding table between them. The night is thick with the smell of fried noodles, charcoal from the street vendors below, and the resin of the old tamarind tree that grows through the terrace floor. The sea sounds like breathing.

Mei talks about the race first. She says Eli’s gybe was too early and he got lucky with the wind shift. She says Kiran’s hull was too stiff for the chop and that is why he pounded. She says Suresh held the wager too long because he wanted to be sure Eli was ready, not because he wanted to see Kiran lose.

Then she says, “I need to tell you something about your parents.”

Eli puts his fork down. The noodles are hot and sweet and he has been hungry since dawn, but he cannot eat while she is looking at him that way.

“Not that they’re dead,” she says. “Everyone has told you that. I need to tell you they are not.”

The silence that follows is not quiet. It is full of the things Mei has been carrying and not saying: the way she flinches when a siren passes, the way she keeps the curtains drawn after seven, the way she paid Shireen’s clinic bill in cash and never mentioned it.

“They were taken alive,” she says. “Your father — Rasel — he was a carrier for something the registry calls a threshold episode. They moved him through a transfer program. Your mother — Maren — she was building a signature for it. She helped design the tools they used to locate him. Then they used the same tools to locate her.”

Eli listens without moving. The sea breathes. The tamarind tree drops a seed on the corrugated roof.

“I am telling you this because you are leaving tonight,” Mei says. “And because I cannot send you out thinking the people who raised you were the people who made you. They were not. They were the people who kept you when the people who made you decided you were a mistake.”

She stands. She clears the plates. She comes back with a small envelope and puts it on the table between them. Inside is a photograph: a woman with Eli’s eyes and a man with a scar across his left knuckle. The woman is smiling. The man is holding a garden spade.

“Your mother took this,” Mei says. “Your father grew the garden you sleep in. That is all I have.”

Eli puts the photograph in the pocket of his jacket. He does not cry. He does not speak. He thanks her in the way that means he will carry this truth without demanding she carry it with him.

When he leaves the terrace, he does not look back. He does not want her to see that he is afraid.

---

### III

Babaji’s motor catamaran is tied at the public pier. It is a working boat, not a racer: broad beam, diesel engine, cabin with a canvas top, and a cargo hold that smells of engine oil and dried durian. Babaji himself is on deck, checking lines. He is a large man with a gray beard and the habit of talking to his engine as if it were a person.

“You won,” he says. Not a question.

“By a skiff length.”

“Good. The manifest is inside. Cash, pass, clinic wire. Suresh sent the confirmation an hour ago.”

Eli steps aboard. There is one other passenger: a woman in a dark work jacket, standing at the rail with her back to him. She turns when she hears the engine start. She has a face that reads people before they speak — not suspicious, not warm, but precise, the way a clerk reads a document. Her hair is pulled back. She carries a case.

“Wren,” Babaji says. “Handler. She gets you through the Stack intake corridor and onto the northbound vessel. After that you are on your own.”

“Handler,” Eli says.

“House Weaver,” she says. “Knotter. Not an extractor. You find your own routes after Meridian.”

She shakes his hand. Her grip is firm. She does not hold it longer than necessary.

They move away from the quay at last light. The Reach falls behind them: the hill, the shrine lights, the tamarind terrace, the garden where Eli slept the night before. He stands at the rail and watches it go. He is not sure he will ever see it again. He is sure he does not deserve to.

Inside the cabin, he opens Babaji’s envelope. The cash is there. The pass is there. The clinic wire confirmation is there. Underneath is a photograph: a hand with a scar across the left knuckle, wearing a silver-and-brass ring, and in the margin, written in a hand Eli does not recognize, the words *same soil*.

He turns the photograph over. There is nothing on the back.

He puts it in his pocket with Mei’s photograph. He stands at the rail until the island is only a dark shape against a darker sea. Then he goes below and sleeps without dreaming.

---

### IV

The squeeze happens before Wren says goodbye.

They are at the marina gate, waiting for the checkpoint to open. The gate is a concrete arch with a scanner on each side and two Mandate officers in the booth. Wren has Eli’s papers in order. She has rehearsed the intake story: worker lane, contract labor, Stack maintenance shift, legitimate manifest. She has told Eli to keep his head down and not make eye contact with the scanners.

The scanner on the left side flags him.

Wren does not panic. She redirects. “Worker lane,” she says to the officer. “Contract labor. He’s with me.” She puts her own pass on the counter. The officer looks at her pass, then at Eli, then at the scanner readout. The scanner readout shows something the officer is not supposed to see: a receiver signature that lit up during the race, when Eli’s body was already moving faster than his mind could track.

The officer reaches for his radio.

Wren steps between him and Eli. She puts her hand on the counter and says something in a language Eli does not fully catch — not the Strait pidgin they have been speaking, but something older, something the Reach islanders use when they do not want the Stack to understand. The officer hesitates. The radio goes quiet.

“Go,” she says to Eli, in the pidgin again. “North gate. Suresh’s boat. Don’t wait for me.”

“You’re coming with me.”

“I have papers that say I belong here. You don’t. Go.”

She puts something in his hand: a small green cord, knotted at both ends. A Weaver’s token. It means she has attested him. It means she has opened a door he did not know existed. It does not mean she is coming with him.

Eli goes through the north gate. He does not look back. He boards Suresh’s boat. The cast-off rope comes loose. The engine turns over. The boat moves into the strait.

He stands at the stern and watches the marina shrink. He cannot see Wren at the gate. He cannot see whether she is still standing there. He does not know that the raid on Babaji’s offices happens twenty minutes later, that Mei is taken in the same action as the flagged carrier, that the terrace is shuttered and the tamarind tree is left untended.

He only knows that the water is getting wider and darker and that the ring photograph is in his pocket and that his hands will not stop shaking.

By the time the boat passes the last buoy, the sun is below the horizon. The first Custodian drone is already in the air. The hunt has started. Eli does not see it yet. He thinks he is still clear.

He is not.

The chapter ends with the receiver opening — not with a sound or a light or a voice, but with a sensation like a door unlatching in a room he did not know he had inside him. The grid flares. Somewhere far to the north, a Custodian operation marks his signature and begins to move.

And in a sealed archive in Meridian City, a file opens with his name on it. The file contains a photograph of his father’s hand, the ring, and a single line of text: *Carrier event confirmed. Transfer to primary target.*

He does not know any of this. He only knows that the sea is loud and the night is cold and he is finally, truly, on his way.

*End of Chapter 1*
