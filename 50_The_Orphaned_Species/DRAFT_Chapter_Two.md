# Chapter Two — Something of Hers

*Canonical Vol I Chapter 2 — the sustained flight: southern escape → Meridian City processing → Straits crossing → Melaka witness collision → asphyxia breach. Ends at the breach; aftermath is Chapter 3. Voice matches `DRAFT_Chapter_One.md` (present tense, close third, Eli). Drafted 2026-07-21 against `31_volume_I_beatsheet.md`, `99_open_questions.md` #1–#3, `36_the_living_web.md` §3a, `14` § three gears. Open drafting choices flagged at the foot of the file.*

---

He doesn't ask why. He moves.

*Out back. Now.* Mei said it without turning from the pass, in the voice she uses for a grease fire, and Eli's body was already off the stool before his mind agreed. Through the service corridor, past the grease trap and the staff toilet, into the alley where the resort keeps its real life — laundry cages, broken pallets, a man asleep upright against a generator housing. Three steps into the sun and the terrace door opens behind him and the voices come out flat and procedural, the way voices go when the people using them have done this in other towns.

"—registry discrepancy in the facility cohort—"

"—Coalition health authority, voluntary screening—"

White coats. Clipboards. The wrong kind of fever check.

He runs. Not because he has decided anything. Because the alley opens onto the promenade, and the promenade is where the Coalition pretends the war is a theme park, and if he stops here he will put Mei's terrace on a list.

The robot dog crosses his path on its morning route — head down, head up, cameras adjusting with that soft tick, as if it has solved him and found the answer boring. Eli almost pats the warm plastic out of habit. Then the green light on its chest blinks once, filing him, and something in his chest goes cold and clean.

He vaults the chess boards under the shade sails. A knight clicks into ruin. He hits the mall entrance as Rina yanks her shutter the rest of the way down and shouts his name in Bengali — "*Roof stairs! Go!*" — and he goes up.

On the third floor Kiran already has a tandem rental half out of the bike cage, two seats, one rusted chain, the kind of thing tourists rent to pretend they're fit. Kiran trains with him every morning: sticks, footwork, the Forest Foundry habit of turning everything into a race.

"Back seat," Kiran says. "Pedal."

"What—"

"Mei got the compound list while the keepers' net still held. White coats at your door." He swings a leg over the front saddle. "That song you stopped humming in the garden? They played it again downstairs. Someone matched your face."

Eli gets on. The frame wobbles. Kiran pushes off hard.

They fly down the ramp into the plaza — past Nico's counter, the cold pizza ovens, Mina and Lopa shrieking with delight because to them this is a game — and cut toward the waterparks. Behind them: scooters. Not tourists. Two white coats, one riding, one hanging off the back with a tablet that keeps losing the map and finding it again in chunks.

"The kiddy cars," Eli says.

Kiran laughs once, breathless, and wrenches left through the arch where the resort keeps its plastic fleet — pedal cars shaped like ducks and trains and one offended turtle, lined up for children who haven't arrived. Eli lands in a yellow duck. Kiran takes the red train beside him. The pedals are too short and the steering is a joke and they pump anyway, knees knocking, plastic shells wobbling across the splash plaza while the first slide spits a cold arc into nothing.

The scooters can't follow — too wide, too stupid, too visibly ridiculous. One rider dismounts and runs.

It should be funny. Some part of Eli notes that it is funny, a home built for children turned inside out and used as a getaway, and the same part notes that he is going to lose all of it by nightfall, and holds both facts at once without dropping either. *Circle one,* he thinks, without thinking it. *The part that tries.*

The children's train comes round the bend on its bright useless loop, bell dinging, one attendant half asleep in the cab. Eli hauls his duck off the rubber path and throws himself into the last car — not a decision, a grab — and Kiran dives in after. The attendant wakes shouting. The train does not stop. It was built for loops, not emergencies.

They ride one full circuit with the runner losing ground on the curves, and roll off at the platform still moving, and sprint the service road that drops to the marina wall.

Eli's lungs burn. His bag is upstairs. His tool roll is in the service bay. Twelve hours ago he had a wage ticket and a garden and people who expected him at the rail. Now he has Babaji's name if a door asks for it, his mother's song under his ribs, and nothing else.

---

Suresh's boat is not one of the clean racing hulls beyond the marina wall. It is low and broad and diesel-stained, already loaded — sacks of rice, two crates of limes, a cooler of fish, and one bundle wrapped so carefully in blue tarp that Eli knows better than to ask. Suresh stands on the dock with a line in his hand and his face doing something tired.

"Don't slow down," he says.

Eli doesn't. He hits the gunwale at a run and Kiran hits it a second behind, both of them tumbling onto the deck as Suresh casts off and the engine catches rough.

Mei appears on the pier with a bag in her hand. She doesn't wave. She throws it — one clean arc — and Kiran catches it at the stern before it can go in the water. Two boiled eggs knock together inside.

The boat pulls into the strait, and Pelangi Reach shrinks behind the marina wall into palms and half-built towers and the bright loop of the train.

"Get below the rail," Suresh says. He looks at Eli. "Both of you." A beat. "I should have sent the band away."

Kiran slides down against a rice sack, grinning, then sees Suresh's face and stops. "I can get off at the next float."

"No," Suresh says. "You can't. Neither of you can." He coils a line without looking at Eli. "The man in Melaka won't send the thing down a wire. Nobody does now, not about this. You want it, you stand in front of him." The coil drops. "He didn't know your name. That's why he used the song. He gave it to the dock band and let it travel. He knew someone would know it."

Eli waits. The water knocks at the hull.

"Your mother came through here. During the worst of it. With a husband — Bengali, Rahman, Arif the gardener. Gone now." Suresh studies the line in his hands. "She ran with the keepers, the ones who hide the children when it comes on them. You were in that net after. That's how you washed up in Pelangi Reach — Arif's boy, no papers, head down." He looks up, finally. "What your net was told is *dead, don't ask.* The man in Melaka knew her when she was still running. Says he has something of hers."

The tingle climbs the back of Eli's neck and into his scalp. The hair stands up along his arms. He doesn't move, and Suresh goes quiet at whatever he sees in Eli's face.

"Where do I find him?"

"Old town, by the river. A chandlery — ship's stores, rope, batteries, grease. Ask once, and only there. Not anywhere else in that city, not now." Suresh brings the engine up a notch. "One door. You hear me? Everything else in Melaka is someone's ledger."

They clear the Johor shallows by mid-morning and the roadstead off Meridian City opens on the port side — and here the sea stops being empty.

A quarantine line strings the channel: floats, a screening pier, a grey cutter idling with its number painted out. Suresh throttles down to nothing and makes them lie flat under the tarp lip while a processing tender works the boat ahead — a ferry running half-full, its passengers filed off one by one through an arch on the pier. Eli watches through a hand's width of gap because he cannot not watch.

The arch is the first machine of its kind he has ever seen up close. People walk through it slow, made to pause on a painted mark, and above them a gantry of sensors leans in like something drinking. Most pass. Two do not. A young man is turned aside by a gloved hand on his sternum — not rough, worse than rough, *routine* — and walked to a second pen where a woman with a tablet is already writing him down before he has said a word. On the pier wall behind them, laminated and rain-curled, a grid of faces. The missing, or the wanted; at this distance the difference is only which office printed the sheet.

So this is it, Eli thinks. The thing the keepers ran from. It doesn't look like a war. It looks like a queue.

The tender swings toward them. Suresh stands, holds up a manifest and a carton of cigarettes, and talks in the bored register of a man who has paid this exact toll a hundred times. A masked officer glances into the cooler, glances at the rice, and finds the dark water past the boat more interesting. The carton changes hands.

"That was a bribe," Eli says, when they're moving again.

"That was hospitality," Suresh says, and brings the engine up, and does not look at the pier again. Eli does. He looks until the arch is a grey line and then nothing, and the shape of it stays behind his eyes longer than it should, the way the song does.

The strait carries them north the rest of the day, past fishers showing little of themselves and one police launch idling with its blue light off. The war has made everyone economical with brightness.

Melaka begins as cranes and roofs, then old walls, then the river mouth opening between buildings that look like they survived by agreeing to be useful. A yellow quarantine banner hangs from the customs shed. Suresh cuts the engine to idle and hands Eli a paper with one word on it and a line beneath.

*Ask once.*

"You carry one crate off and you walk," Suresh says. "You don't wait for me. You don't say my name in that city." He almost says something else, and doesn't, and that is how Eli knows the man is frightened for him.

---

The city has learned to move around its wound.

Stalls are open under tarps. Tourists haven't come back in numbers but the signs wait for them anyway, bright and patient, promising river cruises and rooms with air-conditioning. A trishaw covered in plastic flowers goes by empty, playing music to no one. Eli carries his crate three streets, sets it where a boy points, and becomes a man with no crate and nowhere to be — which in a reopening port is its own kind of flare.

The chandlery is off a lane too narrow for a van that a van uses anyway. Rope hangs in coils from the ceiling; nets, hooks, sealed batteries, cheap knives in blister packs, tins of grease. A boy of twelve or thirteen does sums behind the counter. From the back a man's voice says, before Eli has spoken, "We don't sell that."

Eli says the word from Suresh's paper.

The pencil stops.

The man comes out wiping his hands. Square, grey, one eye clouded white. He reads Eli's face, then his hands, then the street behind him. "Wrong shop," he says, and it does not feel like a refusal. It feels like a test someone forgot to explain.

"My mother's name was Maren," Eli says. "Rahman was my father's name — the one she used in her work. English. She came through here running with the keepers."

The man's face does not change, and that is how Eli knows it has landed.

Outside, a van passes the mouth of the lane. White, clean, a blue medical seal on the door, rolling slow enough that Eli can see the driver not looking into the shop.

The man watches it in a small mirror nailed above the fishing line. "The one you came for moved this morning," he says. "Said something lit up on the river steps. He didn't want to be where the first person to walk in asking after the music could find him." He takes a receipt pad and writes an address so fast the numbers are almost nothing, tears it off, tucks it under a coil of thin green cord. "You buy cord."

Eli buys cord. The boy rings it up with hands that shake once.

Outside, Eli walks two turns before he reads it. *Old hotel. Fourth floor. Back stair.* And beneath, in smaller writing:

*If a woman tells you to run, run.*

---

By late afternoon the rain comes hard and brief, hitting the street like thrown gravel, then leaving steam rising off the drains.

Two streets from the hotel he sees why the chandlery man moved his witness. Two health workers have set a folding table by the bridge — one taking names, one watching a screen — with a soldier beside them pretending not to be. Down at the river steps a white van waits with its rear doors shut. They aren't checking everyone. They're drawing a net around one approach.

The tingle in his hands goes sharp, as if the skin has learned to listen.

At the next corner someone catches his wrist, and he turns with the old movement — thumb line, elbow, step in — and the woman lets go before he can take the joint.

"Don't," she says. A little older than him, maybe not by much. Short hair under a cap, rainwater on one cheek, a courier bag across her chest. Nothing about her says police. Nothing about her says safe. "The front of the hotel is watched. The river steps are worse."

"Who are you?"

"Someone trying to get another person out of the same building." Her eyes drop once to his hands. "They're not checking for fever. They're watching for activation. Hands, head, whatever lights up."

The charge in his palms gives one hard pulse, and she feels him feel it — he sees her see it — and her face closes like a door.

Down the covered way, the man at the screen looks up.

She takes a cigarette packet from her bag and puts it in Eli's hand. Inside: no tobacco, only paper, stale mint, and a flat strip of notched plastic under the foil. "Laundry door, back lane. The keycard won't work, the shim will. Fourth floor. If you keep moving, they follow you, and I get four minutes for my extraction."

"And if I don't?"

"Then they follow me, and you walk into the front door like an idiot ten minutes later." Then she smiles past him, bright and false, and lifts a hand and says, loud, "There you are — you left your change."

Eli has spent enough of his life being nobody to know when someone has handed him a part. "Sorry," he says, and takes the packet, and doesn't look back until the corner. When he does, she's already walking toward the health table, shoulders loose, giving them someone else to read, and doing it well enough to make him angry.

Four minutes. He runs.

---

The hotel is red under the dirt, half its windows boarded, half hung with laundry lines. The shim opens the laundry door because the lock is old and insulted by effort. Inside smells of bleach and wet cloth. On the second landing a child eats rice in front of cartoons on a cracked phone, looks at him, and points up with the spoon when Eli puts a finger to his lips.

The fourth-floor corridor is open to the river air. From here he can see the box the chandlery man meant — table, soldier, van, two men under the shut tour office's awning. Waiting for one route and one shape. His.

At the end of the corridor a door stands open. A man sits on the floor with his back against the bed. Thin, soaked through, one hand pressed to his ribs, his hair white at the temples and black everywhere else as if fear started there and ran out of patience. Beside him on the bed, threaded on a fieldwork cord gone almost black from handling, a ring: two metals, silver and brass, joined at a seam no one ever filed smooth.

Eli knows it is his father's work the way you know a hand you were fed by. Arif's join, the unfiled seam. His mother's thumb worrying that seam when she was thinking.

The man looks up, and the relief on his face is so naked that Eli hates him for it.

"You brought them with you," Eli says.

"You have her mouth." The man coughs, or laughs. "Hassan. My name — later there won't be time. Listen. You followed the music. So did they. If you take that up here, now, you waste everything she spent to put it in your reach."

"What is it?"

"Her wedding ring. Your father's work. And the thing they tuned the song to — enough of her *in* it to make you forget there's a living man in the room." He swallows. "Your mother left one instruction for the day this found you. The living person over the ring. Me if you can. Anyone if you can't. Not the ring. Not first."

Below, in the street, someone shouts. The charge is no longer only in Eli's hands. It's behind his eyes, under his skin, everywhere. The ring pulls at him the way the song did — *same soil,* her voice, low; her palm on his forehead; the softened rice passed mouth to mouth because he was too small to chew. The first language. Care, direct, no system between. Her, in his hand, if he just closes it.

Boots hit the stairs below. More than one. Fast now.

Eli reaches for Hassan's arm instead of the ring.

"Up," he says.

Hassan stares at him like he didn't think anyone would actually do it. "She told you—"

"I heard you. Move."

He gets under the man's shoulder — lighter than he looks, and smelling of blood and river rain — and they make the hall as the door below hits the wall. The ring is still on the bed. Eli takes it in the last half-second, not first, a fist closing on cord as they go, and even that late it feels like theft and like coming home at once.

The roof hatch sticks, then gives. Rain, river air, the health table and the van far below like toys. He boosts Hassan through and follows and pulls the hatch shut on the noise coming up the stairs. The roofs lean close here. Hassan cannot run. Eli half-carries him over the first gap, the second—

—and his hands flare, the same light that lit the river steps this morning, and across the tiles a woman in a white vest looks up from her thermometer table and points.

"Down," Hassan gasps. "Not up. They'll box the roof."

A fire escape drops toward the back lane. Eli goes first to clear it, then comes back — and the tile behind them is already loud with boots that move in a trained rhythm, and at the foot of the ladder the lane is empty for exactly three heartbeats before the engines turn in from both ends.

They aren't trying to arrest him. They're clearing the lane.

The first operator is off the scooter and closing before Eli is set. He reads the line — angle, range, entry, the shape his body has drilled a thousand mornings — and it is *not enough*, because reading a line is not the same as being three men wide, and the lane is too narrow for the Kali to open the way it wants. A stick catches his wrist. A second catches his ribs. He turns a blade on his forearm and takes a shallow cut, heat then cold, and behind him two of them have Hassan by the arms and are walking him backward toward the van, unhurried, the way you carry something you have already been paid for.

"Hassan—"

"Not the ring," Hassan says, over a shoulder, almost gentle. "*You.* You're the door. Don't let them—"

And then the first operator's hands are on Eli's throat, and the world tilts, because pressure at the neck reaches the old fracture faster than anything else.

He does not feel the brick go out from under his heels. He feels the water first — the black gap by the quay wall, rain and diesel — and then a hard shove between the shoulder blades, and he goes over, and the cord tears out of his fist on the lip of the wall.

He surfaces from the world into no air.

---

The first thing is the lack of air. The second is the cold finding the cut in his forearm as if it has been waiting there. He is strong enough to kick and strong enough to reach, but the hands on his throat are not the hands of someone who will get tired, and the water at the quay gap is not deep enough to drown in. It is only deep enough to hold him under.

The old fracture hums. Names blur into names he does not know. His mother's hum goes empty and comes back as a woman's laugh he has never heard. A fire-escape ladder rings once, metal on stone, and — this is new, this is wrong — it rings *twice*, once here and once somewhere he has never been.

Then the light is under his skin.

It is not a vision. That is the first true thing about it and the only thing he will be sure of afterward: nothing appears, nothing speaks. It is a sense turning on the way an eye opens in the dark — the whole space around him suddenly *there*, present as a second body, and far too much of it at once. Every signature his palms ever misread — heat, tingling, pressure — floods up his arms together and settles behind his eyes and in the base of his skull, sharp and loud, and does not resolve into anything. It is all edges and no picture. A roar with a shape he cannot hold.

He has felt one edge of this his whole life, in the garden, in his hands over a sick man's chest — *reception,* the tower called it, and taught him to fear the word. He never once felt the whole. The whole is not a gift. The whole is drowning twice.

For a moment the roar almost holds still, and in the stillness one thing surfaces, ordered where everything else is noise — not a word, not a face: three rings, one inside the next inside the next. Then the water takes it back.

He is below the lane, looking up through diesel sheen, and he cannot tell where his skin leaves off. A kitchen he has never walked hums with the same hunger as the one under his hands at home. Rain on this roof is rain in a chamber that has no place for him. None of it is inhabited. They are not people. They are moments pressed into stone and left there — a grief set into a foundation when the foundation was new, a breath taught to a circle before the circle was broken — and they do not know him. They know only that something living has taken hold of the wire, and they answer the way anything answers a bell.

He is not seven people. He does not think, even for the length of the thought, that the dead are him. He thinks, with the one part of him still keeping count: *there are too many, and they can all feel me now.*

Somewhere above the water a hook scrapes stone.

---

The surface breaks open around a length of boat hook, and a hand fists in his collar, and the river gives him up.

The woman hauling him over the gunwale is broad and strong and swearing steadily in Malay, and she is nobody he has seen — not the courier, not white-coated, no charge coming off her at all. A fish seller. Her deck is scaled and stinking and the best thing he has ever lain on. She rolls him, drives the heel of her hand under his ribs until the river comes up out of him in a rush, and says, when he can cough, "Breathe, stupid. Breathe now, thank me later."

The lane above them is chaos he can't parse — the operators regrouping, the van pulling out with its rear doors already shut on whatever it took, the health table abandoned mid-name. Hassan is gone. The ring is gone, torn away on the wall or sunk in the gap or in a pocket that is now inside the van; it does not matter which, because all three mean the same thing.

"You're loud," the woman says — not the noise; she means the light still bleeding off him, the way the whole quay seems tuned to him now. She says it the way you'd tell a man he's bleeding. "You were dark an hour ago. Whole river felt you turn on." She spits over the side. "They'll have felt it in rooms you've never been to."

He tries to stand. His legs are not entirely his. The soldier's name from the box below is on his tongue and it is not his name and he does not remember learning it.

On the quay above, the courier woman reappears at the wall — cap gone, face no longer bored, no longer performing anything. She looks down at him on the reeking deck with an expression he will spend a long time failing to name, somewhere between fury and recognition.

"I lost my extraction when you lit up," she says. It is not quite an accusation. It is worse: it is a fact she is deciding what to do with. "So now we're both running."

Behind her the boots find the rhythm again, coming down the lane from both ends.

The fish seller shoves an oar into Eli's hands. "You pull that," she says, "or you swim." And Eli — hunted now, lit up, the ring lost and the man lost and a door standing open inside him that he does not know how to shut — takes the oar, because the only thing left that he knows how to do is keep moving.

He pulls.

## Draft choices — flagged for veto

Four calls I made where canon left room. Each is easy to reverse.

1. **⟦DC-1⟧ Meridian City as a processing checkpoint glimpsed from Suresh's boat** (§ "They clear the Johor shallows"). The restructure lock names a "brief Meridian City ring discovery," and `00_NARRATIVE_STRUCTURE.md:43` calls it "processing" — but the source prose skips Meridian entirely, and the Three-Circles Tablet is a *Chapter 7* artifact. I read "ring discovery" as **first sight of the control apparatus** (the registry arch, the sorting pen, the wall of faces) — *not* the tablet, and not the wedding ring — so the artifact chain stays intact. If you meant Eli should physically *transit* Meridian (disembark, be processed) rather than watch it from cover, or if "ring" meant the wedding ring surfacing earlier, this beat gets rewritten.

2. **The three-rings fragment at the breach** (§ breach). `99 #2` permits "one ordered fragment may surface (three concentric circles)." I took the option because it plants the Three-Circles grammar wordlessly before the Meridian tablet names it. Cut it and the breach is pure undifferentiated noise, which is also canon-clean.

3. **Salmah unnamed on the page.** Canon names the fish-seller rescuer **Salmah** and later has the Custodians seize her boat. I kept her *unnamed here* (she's a stranger to Eli in the moment; naming her is Ch 3 work) and did **not** yet dramatize the boat seizure. If you want her named in-scene or the cost seeded now, say so.

4. **The ring: he takes it "not first," then loses it anyway.** To honor both the locked Manual-Override seed (*choose the living over the ring*) and `99 #2` (*loses both Hassan and the object*), I had Eli reach for Hassan first and grab the ring only in the last half-second — then have it torn away at the wall. This keeps the moral proof (person before object) while still stripping him of both. Alternative: he leaves the ring untouched on the bed entirely (cleaner morally, but then it's captured rather than lost in the water, which changes who has it in Vol II).

**Also folded in, silently, as canon corrections to the source prose** (flagging so you know they're deliberate, not drift): the water-rescuer is the fish seller (Salmah), **not** the chandlery man/boy as the old Ch 3 had it; the witness is **named Hassan and is retaken**, not rescued to the roof; the breach is **field-noise, not "seven chambers"** (the old source used the deprecated seven-lives framing — `36` §3a); and the whole thing is in present-tense close third, **not** the King-James "Elmarillion" register the old Ch 3–4 drifted into.
