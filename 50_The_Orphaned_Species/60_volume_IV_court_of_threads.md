# Volume IV — *The Court of Threads* — Architecture Lock

> **STATUS — REVISED 2026-07-29:** The series is a four-volume arc. Book III
> **does** complete Eli's first relinquishment — not the final civic settlement
> (the teahouse/garden/birds coda still belongs to Book IV), but a real,
> completed constitutional transfer that holds for a genuine stretch of time,
> with the Weavers and men's cloisters elevated into civic power through
> building it rather than surviving an attack on it. Book IV opens by
> summarizing that working stretch, then a coordinated attack on the *working*
> settlement forces the reconstituted institutions to decide, deliberately and
> on the record, whether to invoke the dormant continuity clause planted at the
> end of Book III and ask Eli to accept renewed bounded authority. Book III
> contains only deniable Rowan/Eli attraction—no sex or romance. Book IV carries
> conscious attraction, repeated sex before romance, Rowan's later decision to
> pursue pregnancy, childbirth, institutional settlement, and Eli's **second**
> and final relinquishment.

*The Court of Threads* is a working title. The architecture below is canon even
if the title changes.

---

## 1. Book III → Book IV hinge

Eli does not decide to keep power the first time, and he does not seize it back
the second. By late Book III he has used real bounded authority, refused forced
synchronization, and completed a constitutional transfer — distributed
credentials, redundant verification, no single ceremony an attack could have
reached all at once. It works. For a real stretch of time — long enough that
the reader and Eli both feel it, not a paragraph of summary that evaporates —
the constituent assembly, the Weavers, and the men's cloisters govern without
him, and he is a private citizen: father-adjacent, useful, ordinary, replaceable.

Book IV opens on that stretch briefly, then breaks it. A coordinated attack
kills, scatters, or compromises enough of the assembly and its officers that no
routine succession can be credibly authenticated — not a strike on an
incomplete process this time, but on a working one, precisely because it is
working and precisely because that costs someone real leverage. Different court
factions exploit different parts of the opening; do not reveal one mastermind.

The compact's dormant continuity clause — stated on the record at the end of
Book III, then not used — can be invoked to restore Eli's bounded mandate until
a successor is safely reconstituted and certified. It does not fire
automatically. The reconstituted institutions **choose** to invoke it,
explicitly, with everyone in the room having already watched the alternative
work — which is the only way "temporary is fine, permanent is not" can be
dramatized honestly rather than asserted. The extension is public, reviewable,
and legally bounded, but every faction that benefits from delay now has a
reason to keep the certification crisis unresolved. Eli's Book IV danger is not
crude seizure. It is becoming the permanent answer to emergencies other people
learn to prolong — a temptation sharper the second time, because everyone
involved, including Eli, now knows exactly how well the alternative can work
and exactly how fragile it turned out to be.

Two civic movements save continuity during the second crisis:

- **The Weavers** evacuate delegates, preserve living attestations, place
  survivors, restore routes, and determine which identities and messages can
  still be trusted.
- **The men's cloisters** hold perimeters, move casualties, repair damaged
  infrastructure, protect assembly remnants, and keep demobilized fighters from
  being discarded or remobilized without limit.

Their crisis response deepens political power they had already earned in Book
III by building the first transfer. Neither rise is granted by Eli, and neither
institution is unified.

Book III plants only retrospectively legible Rowan/Eli charge. Eli looks for
Rowan's political reaction, she can steady or confront him without triggering
defense, and one practical touch may briefly alter their attention. Every signal
must retain a complete nonromantic explanation. No explicit desire, near-kiss,
jealous Wren, prolonged body description, or dialogue announcing another life.
Book III ends with no Rowan/Eli sex, no Rowan/Eli romance, and no pregnancy.

## 2. POV and tonal change

Book IV is led through **women's perspectives**, with **Rowan and Wren as the
principal recurring lenses**. Asha may carry the institutional/audit lens;
Sora, Qiao, Maren, or another already-established woman may receive a bounded
away movement only when she owns a consequential choice Rowan and Wren cannot
witness. Do not turn the volume into a catalogue of female viewpoints.

Eli is no longer the home consciousness. The reader experiences the ruler,
lover, symbol, and prospective father largely from outside. This makes his
public gravity more legible and prevents Rowan's pregnancy from becoming
primarily his emotional experience.

The governing register is **subtle power**:

- access before command;
- seating before battle lines;
- delayed messages before open attacks;
- medical privacy before bodily capture;
- household recognition before succession law;
- placement, hospitality, rumor, ceremony, and attestation as instruments of
  rule.

Book III asks who commands during attack. Book IV asks who reaches the commander,
who prepares the room, whose version enters the record, and who is made to wait.

## 3. The court and the two rising institutions

The emergency court forms around Eli because the constituent assembly cannot yet
replace him. It is not a restored monarchy, although monarchic language and
behavior accrete around it.

### The Weavers

Weaver power comes from social infrastructure: household continuity, routes,
placement, welcome, identity attestation, trusted messages, medical and
reproductive privacy, and knowledge of who is actually answerable for whom.
Wren's Knotter craft becomes court power because rooms, audiences, residences,
introductions, and safe placements determine what the ruler can know.

The Weavers divide over:

- remaining a quiet mutual-aid ecology versus accepting public office;
- living attestation versus scalable registry;
- secrecy versus appeal and review;
- protecting Rowan versus claiming authority over her pregnancy;
- whether emergency access rules should become permanent law;
- whether intimacy with Eli gives Wren or Rowan improper influence.

Asha presses for consent, public limits, independent abort contacts, return
rights, audit, and appeal before humane custom hardens into unreviewable state
power. The Weavers may retain civic influence, but they may not become a hidden
sovereign or the sole source of legitimate identity.

### The men's cloisters

The cloisters enter Book IV with earned power from Book III: veterans, former
Extras, Harbor Thread elders, repair crews, workshops, defensive companies,
public loyalty, supply capacity, and the memory of holding communities together
under attack. Many men regard Eli as founder, brother, and the first ruler who
treated them as citizens rather than expendable bodies.

The cloisters divide among:

- constitutional houses seeking civic standing without permanent
  militarization;
- veterans who believe only Eli can keep them from becoming disposable again;
- former Extras who distrust household and Weaver authority;
- mutual-aid houses centered on work, grief, disability, repair, and continued
  obligation;
- ambitious commanders tempted to convert representation into personal force;
- Sol-influenced chapters offering masculine sovereignty and faster certainty.

Their Book IV test is whether a men's keeping-order can resist becoming a
praetorian guard, spending-machine, or mating/acceptability authority. They must
transfer loyalty from Eli's person to a civic compact without surrendering the
standing they earned.

### No sex-bloc simplification

Do not map all women onto the Weavers or all men onto the cloisters. Neither
movement speaks for a sex, and both contain alliances, dissent, class conflict,
regional obligations, and people who belong to neither. Their rivalry is
structural: one controls much of trust and passage; the other much of defense
and repair.

### The inherited labor inversion

Women already hold much of the work the old world coded male: freight, grid and
water maintenance, construction, convoy leadership, perimeter defense, drone
operation, enforcement, repair, and command. War killed or conscripted many men;
the postwar settlement then classified many survivors as house dependents,
deniable Thread labor, or Extras while continuing to demand the same functions.
Women inherited the vacancies without being relieved of pregnancy, domestic,
sexual, or caregiving burdens.

Book IV treats this as lived social structure rather than a slogan. Some women
love the work and defend authority they earned. Some want relief from compulsory
danger. Some fear the cloisters will use restored male standing to displace
them. Some cloister chapters mistake dignity for entitlement to “men's work.”
Some Weaver houses preserve female control by continuing to classify unattached
men as presumptive threats.

The settlement may not solve this by restoring traditional roles or by declaring
sex irrelevant to every bodily circumstance. Work and dangerous service become
answerable to aptitude, training, consent, rotation, recovery, appeal, and
continuing care. Rowan's pregnancy changes her own present capacities and risks;
it does not become an argument about what all women should do.

## 4. The courtly-intrigue engine

The attack that extended Eli's mandate remains an active mystery. Competing
factions may have enabled, exploited, or concealed parts of it for different
reasons; avoid one mastermind explaining the whole court.

Recurring intrigue must operate through concrete stakes:

- certification evidence disappears or arrives through a compromised route;
- an audience list changes and therefore changes policy;
- a survivor is placed in a household that controls her testimony;
- a cloister commander withholds security unless granted formal standing;
- women officers and cloister veterans contest appointments, seniority, risk
  rotation, and whether restored male standing means female displacement;
- a Weaver attestation is challenged as private favoritism;
- a medical record or pregnancy rumor is leaked;
- marriage, consort, paternity, and household language are used to force the
  plural relationships into hierarchy;
- factions delay the assembly while publicly insisting they protect continuity;
- an attempted protection of Rowan becomes surveillance or confinement;
- ceremonial precedence is used to manufacture a successor before law does.

The court's strongest pro-dynasty faction should have a credible case: a named
heir and formal household might prevent civil war. Its error is converting
persons and pregnancy into constitutional instruments.

## 5. Romance architecture — attraction, sex, romance

Book II ends with two continuing romances centered on Wren:
Wren/Rowan and Wren/Eli. Rowan/Eli is then affectionate and politically
significant but not yet romantic.

Book III gives their direct political bond a faint, deniable charge. It should
read first as trust, attention, coordination, and the unusual ease with which
each can challenge the other. Book IV's Rowan POV reveals that physical
awareness existed inside moments the reader could reasonably understand as
political intimacy.

Early in Book IV, Rowan and Eli consciously recognize direct attraction and have
consensual sex on several separate occasions **before** either treats the bond as
romantic. They have sex because they want one another—not because Wren is absent,
withholding, or being replaced. Each encounter requires present consent and
material disclosure under the existing plural agreements. Sex grants no status,
claim, exclusivity, access to Wren, promise of continuation, or presumption of
parenthood.

Repeated sex becomes bodily familiarity; political work, disagreement,
post-sex conversation, sleep, care, missing one another, and private language
then make the relationship romantic. The sequence must remain visible:
**attraction → sex → repeated intimacy → romance**. Wren neither brokers nor
authorizes the bond.

Once the romance has emerged, the household contains **three distinct romantic
dyads**, not one undifferentiated group relationship:

- **Wren / Rowan — recognition:** formative ply trust, shared Weaver grammar,
  recovered desire, and embodied history.
- **Wren / Eli — discovery:** emotional naming, grounding, chosen tenderness,
  and home made deliberately in the present.
- **Rowan / Eli — construction:** public partnership, accountable power,
  generational hope, and the dangerous work of imagining a future together.

The court tries to rank these bonds because hierarchy simplifies inheritance.
It casts Rowan as consort/mother, Eli as sovereign/father, and Wren as mistress
or official. The three must not answer by pretending their bonds are identical.

Book II gives Eli jealousy when Rowan's return makes him feel provisional.
Book IV gives Wren a related but different fear: pregnancy and the
Rowan/Eli biological bond appear able to create permanence, public recognition,
and a future from which she can be excluded. Wren's resolution is not automatic
co-parenthood or equal maternity. Her place in the child's life must be asked,
named, and consented to separately from either romance.

## 6. Rowan's motherhood desire and pregnancy

Rowan knows she wants to become a mother before she becomes Eli's lover. This is
an independent life desire, not a response to his authority, Wren's
availability, the baby bust, or court pressure. Wanting motherhood does not mean
she has selected Eli as father or chosen a date for conception.

During the early sexual relationship Rowan actively does not want pregnancy and
uses ordinary reliable contraception. She may tell Eli plainly that she wants
children eventually and that their sex is not how she is choosing one. Only
after their relationship has become romantic does Rowan decide whether she wants
to raise a child specifically with Eli. That co-parenting decision is distinct
from sexual consent, romantic commitment, and Wren's separate future role.

During Book IV, Rowan deliberately chooses when to begin trying to conceive.
Conception and discovery occur in Book IV, early enough for gestation and birth
to complete within the volume. The court may already be speculating about
consort and heir before conception; its attempt to define the relationship
faster than the people inside it is part of the intrigue.

Rowan is the primary authority over pregnancy, medical disclosure, attendants,
movement, intervention, and acceptable risk. Pregnancy does not make her passive
or magically protected. It materially changes her work: nausea, fatigue, food,
balance, sleep, pain, appointments, route duration, danger tolerance, and later
mobility must affect scenes.

She remains a capable Weft while adapting rather than denying her body. Other
people may offer necessary care; the conflict begins when care becomes custody.
Eli's fatherhood gives him responsibility and a voice in their relationship,
not jurisdiction over Rowan's body. Wren's ply history gives her knowledge and
trust, not automatic medical access.

In a baby-bust society and a messianic court, the pregnancy becomes contested
public property:

- demographic hope;
- proof of divine or genetic continuity;
- a stabilizing heir;
- a target for registry and security;
- leverage over Eli;
- a reason to formalize Rowan and demote Wren;
- a bridge supposedly joining the Weavers and men's cloisters by blood.

The text rejects all of these ownership claims without treating pregnancy,
motherhood, or freely chosen continuity as suspect.

## 7. Climax — birth and relinquishment

Eli prepares the legal and practical transfer throughout Book IV. He does not
abandon public duty impulsively when labor begins. Wren, Rowan, Asha, the
constitutional cloisters, assembly survivors, and other communities make a
credible successor structure possible before the final crisis.

As Rowan enters labor:

- a court faction moves to recognize the unborn or newborn child as Eli's heir;
- the reconstituted assembly reaches its succession vote;
- pro-Eli cloister chapters threaten to reject any commander not personally
  chosen by him;
- Weaver leaders must decide whether their living attestations will certify a
  dynasty, certify the assembly, or refuse sole control of legitimacy.

The birth pays off the three bonds without turning Rowan into scenery:

- Rowan controls the room, attendants, information, and interventions.
- Wren returns to the oldest ply function: Rowan can lower her guard while Wren
  faces outward.
- Eli grounds Rowan and remains present as lover and father rather than leaving
  to reclaim the center.

When officials demand an override or extension, Eli activates the prepared
transfer and trusts the distributed institutions to govern without him. The
cloisters accept civilian, plural command; the Weavers certify a reviewable
constitutional process rather than a bloodline and accept limits on their own
attestation power.

**Before the child takes a first breath, the crown has ceased to be Eli's.**
The child is born without title, office, prophecy, or inherited claim.

---

## 8. End state

- Eli is a father and citizen, not permanent ruler.
- Rowan is a mother by choice, not consort, vessel, or demographic asset.
- Wren's relationship to the child is explicitly negotiated; romance alone does
  not assign parenthood.
- All three romances remain distinct and chosen.
- The Weavers retain public civic influence under audit, appeal, and no monopoly
  on legitimate identity.
- The men's cloisters retain standing, houses, memory, repair work, and
  consent-bound defense without becoming Eli's personal army.
- The constituent system distributes authority across communities and
  institutions capable of surviving Eli's absence.
- No heir resolves the work of coexistence.

Governing lines:

> Power is not only who gives the order. It is who prepares the room in which
> the order becomes possible.

> A child may continue a life. A child may not be made to continue a crown.

## 9. Governing ten-chapter spine — LOCKED 2026-07-28

Book IV spans roughly ten months. The first four chapters cover the court's
first six to eight weeks; Chapter 5 discovers the pregnancy; Chapters 6–8 use
explicit seasonal and bodily time jumps; Chapters 9–10 cover labor, transfer,
birth, and the later ordinary coda. The calendar must be legible through weather,
food, clothes, clinical milestones, construction, and changed work—not chapter
labels alone.

### Chapter 1 — The Doors *(Wren)*

- **Immediate want:** reconstitute certification without allowing the Weavers'
  emergency custody of living attestations to become permanent hidden
  sovereignty.
- **Court engine:** audience lists, lodging, message order, food, transport, and
  access determine which account of the attack reaches Eli. Union continuity
  officials, Weaver houses, cloister councils, Sol-aligned men, registry
  families, and local hosts all contest the first formal seats.
- **Attack inquiry:** evidence proves overlap rather than one mastermind:
  stolen Union continuity credentials enabled the false key; a Sol-linked cell
  supplied the forced-unity counterfeit; a private security contractor targeted
  the Sideways lab; at least one unknown actor exploited all three.
- **Wren's choice:** she gives Asha's objection ledger to a review body with
  non-Weaver members and appeal authority. Cost: the Weavers lose exclusive
  control of the evidence that made them indispensable.
- **Relationship:** Wren and Eli remain lovers, but court attempts to treat her
  access as office. She refuses consort privileges and accepts a reviewable
  public route role on her own authority.
- **End hook:** Rowan's western delegation arrives with a living attestation
  capable of disqualifying a favored successor—and an audience list designed
  to keep her from Eli.

### Chapter 2 — Present Consent *(Rowan)*

- **Immediate want:** deliver the western attestation and prevent the court from
  converting her access to Eli into proof of either corruption or consort
  status.
- **Political action:** Rowan gets the evidence into the record through a public
  route, disqualifying a continuity official who concealed cipher access. The
  removal delays succession but prevents a compromised handover.
- **Attraction:** Rowan recognizes that her attention to Eli is bodily as well
  as political. They name mutual attraction directly and disclose it under the
  agreements that already govern the three existing dyads.
- **First sex:** chosen because they want one another. Wren is available,
  continuing both her relationships, and neither broker nor permission-giver.
  Rowan states that she wants to be a mother someday, is not choosing pregnancy
  now, and is using reliable contraception.
- **Guard:** sex grants no audience, vote, title, promise, household rank,
  access to Wren, or presumption of repetition.
- **End hook:** a court scheduler records Rowan leaving Eli's rooms and quietly
  changes her next audience category.

### Chapter 3 — Three Rooms *(Wren)*

- **Immediate want:** keep the court from ranking the bonds after the sexual
  relationship becomes visible.
- **Court engine:** factions cast Rowan as future consort, Wren as mistress or
  chamber official, and Eli as a monarch whose private household should clarify
  succession. Weaver rivals argue Wren's disclosure rules make her politically
  compromised.
- **Romance action:** Rowan and Eli have sex on several separate occasions under
  present consent. Wren experiences jealousy not as fear of losing either
  romance alone, but as fear that the Rowan/Eli bond can acquire public
  permanence from which she is structurally excluded.
- **Choice:** Wren refuses both secrecy and a staged equal-triad performance.
  The three state publicly that the dyads are distinct and confer no office.
- **Institutional action:** Wren helps create separate residence, audience, and
  conflict-of-interest rules for intimate partners of officeholders.
- **End hook:** an altered medical-access list appears before any pregnancy
  exists, proving the court is already building an heir pathway around Rowan.

### Chapter 4 — What We Build *(Rowan)*

- **Immediate want:** determine whether repeated physical intimacy with Eli has
  become a life relationship rather than a protected exception.
- **Romance turn:** work, disagreement, sleep, care, humor, missing one another,
  and private language make Rowan/Eli romantic. They name it after the change,
  not before.
- **Motherhood decision:** Rowan distinguishes four choices aloud: wanting
  motherhood, loving Eli, choosing him as a possible co-parent, and choosing
  when to try. She and Eli agree they want to raise a child together. Wren is
  asked separately what relationship, if any, she wants with a future child;
  she does not answer under pressure.
- **Political action:** Eli completes a credible transfer package: distributed
  certification, rotating coordination, Weaver audit and appeal, civilian
  control of cloister defense, and no dynastic succession.
- **Choice:** Rowan deliberately stops contraception only after medical
  consultation and a private waiting period. Sex remains sex, not a conception
  ritual.
- **End hook:** the attack inquiry recovers proof that several factions delayed
  certification after the strike because Eli's extension benefited them.

### Chapter 5 — The Rumor *(Asha, then Wren across a hard break)*

- **Time:** several weeks later.
- **Immediate want (Asha):** audit the Weavers' court power while recovering
  from the assembly wound.
- **Institutional reveal:** a senior Weaver protected witnesses by rerouting
  them, but also delayed Amina Cole's return because her testimony would have
  broken the continuity crisis sooner. Protection and self-entrenchment coexist.
- **Pregnancy discovery:** Rowan privately confirms pregnancy. She controls who
  is told, including Eli and Wren, and sets medical disclosure terms.
- **Leak:** appointment metadata—not Rowan's medical record—reaches a
  pro-dynasty faction. Rumor outruns confirmation.
- **Wren's choice:** she protects Rowan's privacy without pretending privacy can
  erase the political danger. She declines automatic co-parent status and asks
  for time to choose a real role.
- **End hook:** the court announces a “continuity celebration” for an heir
  Rowan has not publicly acknowledged.

### Chapter 6 — The Body of State *(Rowan)*

- **Time:** late first trimester into second.
- **Immediate want:** continue political and route work while adapting honestly
  to nausea, fatigue, food aversion, clinical monitoring, and changed risk.
- **Court engine:** security uses pregnancy to justify route restrictions;
  Weaver protectors request medical access; cloister loyalists seek a formal
  household; demographic officials call the pregnancy public hope.
- **Choice:** Rowan refuses a dangerous journey rather than performing
  invulnerability, then performs indispensable work remotely and through a
  trusted team. Adaptation is competence, not diminishment.
- **Conflict:** an attempted protection becomes confinement. Wren helps Rowan
  leave through ordinary access craft; Eli supports her authority over the body
  even when afraid.
- **Labor inversion:** women officers and cloister veterans negotiate rotation,
  seniority, care work, and dangerous assignments without restoring sex caste.
- **End hook:** a credible threat targets Rowan's medical route, while evidence
  suggests the threat may be intended to justify—not execute—permanent custody.

### Chapter 7 — The Two Houses *(Wren)*

- **Time:** middle pregnancy.
- **Immediate want:** keep Weaver and cloister power from hardening into rival
  sex blocs around the pregnancy.
- **Court engine:** constitutional cloisters demand formal standing; a
  praetorian faction pledges personal loyalty to Eli and the unborn child;
  Weaver houses split over registry-like living attestation and protective
  custody.
- **Wren's relationship to the child:** after time and direct conversation with
  Rowan and Eli, Wren chooses a named role of continuing adult/kin—not automatic
  mother, not outsider, and not constitutional guardian. Exact household terms
  remain revisable.
- **Choice:** Wren brokers a two-house settlement with cross-membership, audit,
  appeal, civilian control, and no authority over mate-worthiness, pregnancy, or
  inherited office.
- **Attack inquiry payoff:** no single mastermind. A continuity official,
  Sol-linked cell, contractor, and self-entrenching Weaver each enabled a
  different layer; the remaining unknown coordination stays open for ordinary
  law rather than final-villain revelation.
- **End hook:** Eli's completed transfer calendar is challenged by cloister
  chapters threatening to reject any successor he does not personally name.

### Chapter 8 — The False Heir *(Rowan)*

- **Time:** third trimester.
- **Immediate want:** finish the succession terms before her mobility and energy
  narrow further.
- **Court engine:** a pro-dynasty coalition presents the strongest case for an
  heir: one named household may prevent civil war, stabilize command, and keep
  the Weavers and cloisters from fighting over legitimacy.
- **Counter:** Rowan refuses to solve institutional mistrust with her child's
  body. Eli publicly disclaims any hereditary claim. Wren proves that living
  attestation can certify a process without certifying blood.
- **Bodily plot:** false labor or a serious late-pregnancy complication forces
  the court to function while Rowan is unavailable. Her prior instructions—not
  Eli's panic—govern care.
- **Transfer readiness:** successor council, distributed credentials, physical
  backups, abort conditions, and defense oaths are complete.
- **End hook:** labor is near; the reconstituted assembly schedules the final
  succession vote for the same week.

### Chapter 9 — The Room Prepared *(Wren)*

- **Immediate want:** prepare a birth room that belongs to Rowan while the court
  prepares a constitutional room designed to claim the child.
- **Labor begins:** Rowan controls attendants, information, intervention, and
  who enters. Wren faces outward in the old ply function; Eli remains inside as
  lover and father.
- **Political crisis:** dynasty delegates move to recognize the unborn child;
  pro-Eli cloisters threaten to reject plural command; Weaver leaders are asked
  to certify blood or withhold attestation.
- **Choice:** Wren releases the final living attestations to the reviewable
  assembly process, ending Weaver monopoly. Constitutional cloisters accept the
  assembly's civilian command and isolate their praetorian chapters.
- **End hook:** officials demand one more continuity extension from Eli while
  Rowan enters active labor.

### Chapter 10 — Before the First Breath *(Rowan, with a hard-break Wren coda)*

- **Climax:** Eli does not leave the birth to seize the political center. From
  Rowan's room, he activates the already prepared transfer. The reconstituted
  assembly certifies plural civilian authority; the Weavers certify the process
  under audit; the cloisters transfer defense loyalty from Eli to the compact.
- **Locked timing:** the crown ceases to be Eli's before the child takes a first
  breath.
- **Birth:** bodily, difficult, and governed by Rowan's prior and present
  choices. No supernatural sign, inherited title, prophecy, or perfect family
  tableau.
- **Immediate aftermath:** the child has a private name before any public
  announcement and no civic status beyond personhood. Rowan is mother by choice;
  Eli is father and citizen; Wren's named kin role begins through care, not
  romantic assumption.
- **Coda, months later:** the shared garden and teahouse material retired from
  Book III lands here. Eli pours tea and takes instruction. Rowan, Wren, and Eli
  retain three distinct romances and ordinary disagreements. The Weavers and
  cloisters retain bounded public roles. Amara remains part of the social world
  without forgiving Eli. Sideways research continues under joint civilian
  custody; no ship launches.
- **Closing image:** mountain swifts leave to feed while Eli works in the garden.
  He is wanted and replaceable. Nobody interprets the birds.

## 10. Book IV guardrails

- Rowan/Eli sex never occurs because Wren is absent, unavailable, or being
  replaced.
- Wren neither grants nor withholds permission for a bond she does not own; the
  existing relationships require disclosure and renegotiation, not brokerage.
- Rowan wants motherhood before choosing Eli, then separately chooses Eli,
  timing, and conception.
- Pregnancy is not discovered later than Chapter 5.
- No court faction is correct that pregnancy creates public ownership, even
  when its stability argument is politically credible.
- Wren's fear of exclusion is not solved by assigning equal maternity.
- The attack has multiple contributors and beneficiaries. Do not collapse it
  into a single mastermind.
- Weavers and cloisters each surrender a monopoly in the climax; neither is
  merely defeated by the other.
- The final transfer is prepared, legal, distributed, and materially viable
  before labor. Birth supplies the deadline, not the solution.
- The child inherits relationships and history, never office.
