## Chapter 17—A Crown With an End [PLACEHOLDER]

Eli walks past the ceremony platform and enters the civic room through the side door.

The crown has ended.

The judgment has not.

Before he sits, Eli asks Wren for the packet. She hands it over without comment: testimony files, route logs, injury reports, Arman's drone footage, Owen's eleven-minute audio, Amara's written statement, and Leilani's empty envelope.

He reads each one twice.

The first pass lets him feel what the witness felt. The second pass lets him look for what the witness did not say.

Amara names the sleep-entry incident and Eli's deciding vote. She does not name the fact that she stood between him and the door. She does not name the moment her own fatigue turned from personal cost into structural warning. She leaves the gap clean enough that someone reading quickly would see guilt.

Owen's audio is a field report on the compact's dispersal rhythm under assault. He records the stations going down, the field fragmenting, the casualties that followed. He does not record the moment before the attack when Sol's field surge nearly collapsed the local one. That moment is missing from the file but present in Eli's own body — the pressure against his palms, the hum he felt before the drones struck. The drone footage shows the impact sequence but not the field overlap. The two files contradict each other without speaking.

Arman's footage is technically complete. It lacks audio from the transmission station's last two minutes. That absence is itself evidence: somebody scrubbed the record. But why those two minutes? What happened in them that required deletion?

Leilani's envelope is empty.

He sits with the contradictions for an hour before the doors open. Wren does not interrupt. Malia does not offer a shortcut. The room lets him work, the way a proper threshold lets a person stand still before crossing.

He writes three questions for himself, not for the record:

1. What did each witness see that they chose not to say?
2. What is missing from every file that was not missing by accident?
3. If the Union's registry teams had predicted the attack, what would they have done differently?

The third question is the one that will open the hearing. Not by accusation. By invitation.

Before the doors open, a Continuity liaison posts a timed notice on the hall's side board: **REINSTATEMENT RECOMMENDATION DUE 17:40** — forty minutes before emergency authority expires. Present-tense job. Deadline. The liaison does not argue ideology; she collects signatures for a memo that would put Eli back at the top of the chart "pending certification risk." Wren removes the notice, photographs it, and files the photograph as evidence of the job itself.

Malia names the hearing hoʻoponopono.

"That is a practice with its own people, relationships, and responsibilities," she says before the doors open. "This is the same thing under a public compact three weeks old. We are here to put something right in relationship, not to hand the work to a sentence."

The Union facilitator removes the word from the program.

"But," Malia says, "if you are not trying to put something right, why are we here?"

No one removes that question.

The civic room was once an agricultural lecture hall. Its seats face a wall where fertilizer charts have been replaced by casualty names, authority logs, route maps, medical costs, and the compact's expiry notice.
