## Chapter 22—The Drone-Strike Cost
The drone strike is not a strike. It is a single pass, low and loud, that drops nothing heavier than a message canister and a frequency jammer. The canister lands in the gutter outside the clinic. The jammer kills the network for eighteen minutes.

During those eighteen minutes, three things happen.

First, the patient in room four flatlines and does not recover. The monitor was on a backup battery. The alarm was audible. The medic in the hallway heard it. He could not get the door open because the jammer scrambled the electronic lock.

Second, the message canister opens when the medic kicks it. Inside is a single sheet of paper, printed in the house's own font, listing the patient's name, the route they traveled, and the person who signed for them at the border. The paper is a receipt. It is also a threat.

Third, Maren, who is sitting in the kitchen because the clinic has no room for her, watches the drone pass overhead and does not flinch. She has seen drones before. She has seen them drop things that were not messages. She has seen them drop things that were.

When the network comes back, the house's ledger shows an entry that no one authorized. It records a debt: one patient, one corridor, one signature. The debt is assigned to Maren's name.

She reads the entry. She does not dispute it. She stands from the kitchen table, walks to the sink, and washes the bowl she was eating from. The water is cold. The bowl is ceramic. The network hums behind the wall.

Bima's threshold house is the second hit. The drone returns at dusk, flying lower, dropping a single munition that lands in the garden plot where he grows the peppers Mei uses for sauce. The house is wood and canvas. It burns fast. The heat melts the ground-fault detectors mounted on the eaves. The smell reaches the clinic in ninety seconds.

Mina is on the porch when it happens. She sees the flash. She does not wait for the sound. She pulls the intake list from the wall and runs.

Three people are inside. Two get out. One does not. The one who does not is a woman named Lita Cruz. She is seventy-two. She has been moving the garden tools into the storage loft because she does not want Bima to come back to rust. She is carrying a shovel. The shovel is heavy. The stair is steep. The fire moves faster than the stair.

Bima finds the shovel in the ash. He holds it for a long time. He does not say anything. He does not cry. He just holds the shovel and looks at the place where the stair used to be.

Mina stands beside him. She does not touch him. She knows the weight of the shovel is the only thing keeping him upright.

Eli arrives. He reads the house ledger. He sees the entry. He sees the debt assigned to Maren's name. He sees the receipt in the gutter. He sees the shovel in Bima's hands.

He does not say anything. He does not offer a compensation package. He does not promise that the compact will rebuild the house. He just stands there and lets the night cool the ash.

Isaiah 43:2: when you walk through fire, you will not be burned. The compact walks through. Bima walks through. Lita Cruz does not.

The network reports seven more attacks in the southern corridor before dawn. The west relay stays down. The clinic runs on backup power. The intake list is rewritten by hand. The receipt stays in the gutter until Mei picks it up with tongs and puts it in a fireproof box. She does not read it again.

Nothing in the world is softer than water. Yet nothing is better at attacking the hard and strong. The drone is the hard. The voluntary network is the soft that survives.

The drone strike costs a named life, a threshold house, and a receipt that will not stay buried.
