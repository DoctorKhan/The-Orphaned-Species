# The Orphaned Species — Manuscript (reconciled draft)

*Volume I — **The Breach**. Present-tense, close third on Eli. Scene-driven: dramatize in real time, let the reader fill in, no establishing blocks or explained interiority. **Voice target:** lyrical but restrained; warm and human; socially funny; sensory-textured; mostly external/cinematic; flowing and contemporary-simple; lightly interpretive; dry in humor; specific with names, jobs, objects, and places. Vary by scene pressure: warmer and more social in Pelangi Reach, sharper and more procedural under pursuit, quieter and stranger around psi. ## Chapter One — The Hour That Belongs to No One

Before light, Pelangi Reach belongs to the machines, the workers, and the people who cannot sleep.

Eli crosses the plaza with the water key in one hand and the hose over his shoulder. Halfway across, something comes out from under the shade sails behind him.

Small feet. Hard feet. Too even for a person trying to be quiet.

He keeps walking for six steps and listens to it follow. Click, click, click on the tile. The plaza is empty except for the chess boards under their awnings, the closed cafe, and the dark glass of the lobby reflecting him back too thin.

Then the thing stops when he stops.

Eli turns.

The robot dog raises its square head, cameras adjusting with a soft little tick, as if it has solved him and found the answer disappointing. Eli lets out the breath he had not meant to hold and pats the warm plastic between its cameras.

"Morning, Inspector."

It leans into his hand with borrowed affection, then remembers the route someone wrote into it and trots on.

Under the shade sails, the chess boards are already playing their little wars. One clicks a knight into ruin. Eli moves the black queen one square.

"Hey," someone calls from the balcony above the closed cafe. "That was mine."

"Then stop losing to furniture," Eli says.

The voice above him laughs. Someone higher up tells them both to shut up in Bengali, and the sound travels down the concrete shaft with the smell of salt, old paint, and someone's breakfast oil.

He takes the stairs. On the third floor he fixes Rina's fire door with the brick she keeps hidden behind the planter. On the fifth he lifts Mrs. Dutta's abandoned sack of potting mix and carries it up because everyone else will walk around it all day, and everyone will notice if he stops being the person who doesn't.

By the time he reaches the ninth-floor garden, his shirt is damp and the first workers are calling to one another below.

The seedlings have come up pale again. He pinches one off at the base and rolls the stem between his fingers. Too thin. He lays it on the concrete lip with the others and works down the row on his knees, checking each one by feel.

The soil is damp. His hands are warm in it.

His father did this part with his eyes shut. Eli tries it with his eyes shut. The plants still come up pale and stop.

A generator catches somewhere below. Out past the rail the other towers stand black and half-built against the sky, and beyond them the water, going out. He can smell mud and frying oil and the sweet rot under the coconut palms. Two men are already in the trees by the promenade, knocking coconuts down into a net while a third stands below and tells everyone not to die in Bengali.

Pelangi Reach is quiet only at this hour. The reopened resort-port is still half unfinished, half refuge. Later the Forest Foundry people will come out with laptops and wet hair, talking companies and countries and the thing they are all building here. The waterparks will start their pumps. The children's train will make its bright useless loop. At night the light-up butterflies will blink in the palms as if the place has decided to be magical by committee. Eli likes it best before it begins. He likes the beach empty. He likes the racing sailboats waiting beyond the marina wall, their masts ticking softly in the dark.

He's humming before he notices. Three notes, then four, the small turn of it. His mother used to hum it. He stops.

Down in the kitchen the first burner lights with a thump.

---

He goes down.

The steel is still wet and the crates are stacked where Suresh's supply boat left them in the night. Eli ties on, takes down his knife, and starts on the long beans. Mince. Flat of the blade. A clean cut through a chilli spine. Over the blade his mind goes quiet, the way it does over the blade and in the dirt and nowhere else.

Mei comes in and turns on the lights he wasn't using. "You sleep up there now?"

"I supervise tomatoes."

"They complain?"

"Not in a language I have."

"Good. Then they are smarter than customers."

She ties up the night's chits and runs a finger down the list the committee sends, the one that says who eats free this week. She crosses off two names. Adds one. The list is fair. He has worked in enough places where nothing was, and he doesn't say so, because Mei would only look at him.

Tio drags the gas in and sets it down too hard, and Mei tells him what she'll do to him if he cracks another regulator. The old man who fishes brings a bucket to the door and sits beside it and waits to be left alone, which is the whole of his wage.

On slow days before shift, Eli walks the mall by obligation more than habit. Rina with the phone cases sees him from three shops away and lifts a brick in both hands, meaning her fire door has slipped again. He fixes it without being asked. Most of the workers speak Bengali when they are tired or annoyed, and Eli speaks enough to make Rina laugh when she calls him a late son of a broken hinge.

Mrs. Dutta's daughters wait between the mannequins with their school socks falling down. Mina asks if England has ghosts. Lopa asks if the robot dog is his personal friend. When Eli says the robot dog has many clients, both girls laugh so hard Mrs. Dutta comes out with pins in her mouth and tells them not to encourage him.

At the pizza place, Nico slides the burned slices into Eli's hands because "staff food is a philosophy." Then, one week, Nico stops in the middle of stretching dough and cannot remember what day it is. After that Mei puts him on the free-meal list without asking permission from anyone.

Pelangi Reach is not safe because nothing breaks here. It is safe because when something breaks, someone notices.

Then the doors open, and the rest of the burners go up, and the hour Eli can stand inside is over.

For a while he's good in it. The wok throws heat up at him and he takes it without thinking, because thinking is what breaks you here. He can hold the pan a beat longer than makes sense and pull it off the flame with his bare hand, and on the line that's all it is, a trick, the cook who doesn't flinch.

The orders come faster. Mei calls them, chalks them, calls them again. Malay, then Mandarin, then a man at the counter wants something in a language nobody has and Mei has anyway, as if she keeps spares under the till. The bell goes. Tio swears at a lid. Two more tickets land before Eli can plate the first.

On the terrace a band starts up for the dinner crowd: two guitars and a drum box, bright shirts, borrowed amplifiers. The reopening committee pays for music now on Fridays, as if a song can prove a place is alive again. Outside the pass window the robot dog pauses by the terrace steps, camera head tilted toward the players. Then it keeps going.

At first the music is only another layer of noise.

Then one guitar finds four notes.

Eli knows them before he knows he knows them.

His mother in the garden. His mother at a sink. His mother humming with her mouth closed because someone in the next room was sleeping.

Then the words go empty.

Mei calls an order and it reaches him as sound and nothing else. He looks up and her face is there, a face he has worked beside for a year, and for a second it will not go together into anyone he knows. The floor tips. He looks down at the pan in his hand and cannot say what is in it.

The hands keep going. That is the trick the blade taught him years ago, on another island, from a man who is also gone now: let the hands work and stand behind them and wait. They know the wok. They turn it, plate it, set it up on the pass, and he comes back to himself somewhere around the next ticket, sweating, with the pass full and Mei watching him in a way she has never watched him before.

Outside the pass window a drummer from the terrace band has stepped off the stage. He is not looking at the crowd. He is looking at the kitchen.

The guitar keeps finding the four notes, over and over in the set, the way a man might knock on a door until someone opened it.

Mei sees Eli see the drummer. Her face does not change. She calls the next ticket louder.

---

By the time the last order clears, the floor is a wreck and the terrace has gone quiet. Tio hoses it down while Eli mops the prep side, moving through the familiar aftermath as if motion alone could keep the song from settling under his ribs.

Suresh comes in to settle the week, supplier and boatman and fixer when something has to move through a port without becoming paperwork. He counts out the chits with Mei at the till, low, the two of them bent over the money, and Eli is not listening because he is never listening. It is how he gets through a room.

Then a few words cross the kitchen and catch in him like a hook.

The English woman. Her song.

He stops mopping.

Suresh has felt it. He does not turn around. He keeps his voice down and his eyes on Mei, like a man who has decided the boy can hear the thing but will not be handed it.

"Somebody paid that band," he says to her. "They weren't here for Friday music. They were watching the room." Mei has gone still. "There's a man in Malacca asking after the English woman. By name. Says he's got something of hers, and he'll only put it in the right hands." He sets his cap on the counter. "There. I've carried it. I'm square. I came to you because they brought the song into the restaurant. Do what you want with it."

Mei doesn't answer for a moment. Then she turns and looks across the kitchen at Eli, who is standing very still with the mop in his hands, and her face shuts like a door.

"Leave it." She says it to him, not to Suresh. "No one brings a song into your workplace after this long to do you a favour. You've got a place here. A door that locks. People who'd stand in front of you." She holds his eyes. "That's more than most."

His mother has been dead ten years.

"Where in Malacca?" Eli says.

Mei doesn't move. "You don't even know what's in it."

"No."

"Then why go?"

He looks at the water through the open door, dark beyond the kitchen lights. His hands are warm on the handle of the mop, always warm, and for once he does not hate the useless thing in him. It is what walked him here.

"Because if I don't, I'll spend another ten years wondering."

Mei locks the till. The click is small and final.

"That's not a reason," she says.

"It's the one I've got."

## Chapter Two — Something of Hers

Suresh's supply boat will go out on the tide. Eli is on the dock before it in the warm dark, waiting with his bag at his feet like a man who has already made the mistake and is only here to learn its price.

Suresh sees him coming and his face does something tired. "I should have said it quieter."

"You told me. Tell me the rest."

Suresh coils a line without looking at him. "He won't send the thing. Won't say a word down a wire — nobody does now, not about this. You want it, you stand in front of him." The coil drops. "He didn't know your name. That's why he used the song. The drummer asked me who the cook was."

"What band?"

"The ones on the terrace. Dock boys, mostly. They didn't know what they were carrying."

"What song?"

Suresh hums it — four notes, badly, flat on the third — and even mangled it is the song from the garden, the one with no name and no country, the one there has never once in Eli's life been a living soul to ask about.

A tingle climbs the back of his neck and into his scalp; the hair stands up along his arms. He does not move. Suresh stops humming and goes quiet at whatever he sees in Eli's face.

"Yeah," Suresh says. "That's what they were told to look for."

Neither of them speaks. The water knocks at the hull.

"Where is he?" Eli says.

"Old town, by the river. There's a chandlery — ship's stores, rope, batteries, grease, that kind of thing. Ask there and they'll know why you came." Suresh picks the line back up. "Don't ask anywhere else. Not in Malacca, not now. One door, and it's that one. You hear me? And if anyone else comes asking here, no one recognized anything."

---

Mei is at the books when he comes in with his bag. She looks at the bag and not at him, which is how he knows she has already done the math and hated every answer.

"Malacca," she says.

"A few days."

"A few days." She writes a number, crosses it out, writes it again. "There's no work in Malacca. There's only people looking for people." Still not looking at him. "A man who sends music because he doesn't know the face. You know what that is. If they found Suresh, they can find the kitchen."

"I know what it is."

She finally looks at him. "You already decided. I heard you last night." A beat. "I'm not talking you out of it."

He doesn't answer.

"Then you eat first," she says. "I'm not sending you off to die hungry."

He eats at the pass with Tio and the old man and Mei, the staff meal, the one nobody pays for. No one says anything about Malacca. That is how he knows they all know. Tio gives him the better piece of fish and calls it an accident. The old man pushes the chilli bowl closer without looking up. Mei puts two boiled eggs in his bag after and tells him not to make a speech.

---

He packs the bag once, in a room too small for a decision to hide anywhere except in his body.

On his phone the travel warnings stack in three languages — naval activity in the strait, temporary document checks, quarantine corridors, two district names he has never been to and should now avoid. Down in the mall someone has muted the war feed, but the red strip still crawls along the bottom of the screen above Nico's counter.

He unpacks the bag.

For ten minutes he is almost a sane person.

He goes down for water before the mall closes.

Rina is pulling her shutter halfway and counting cash into a tin. "Eli," she calls in Bengali. "Tomorrow early. I saved you the charger with the long cord — the good one, not the tourist one."

"Tomorrow," he says.

Mina and Lopa come skidding out from behind the tailor's counter before their mother can catch them. "Tomorrow you walk with us past the dog," Mina says. "Lopa is scared."

"I am not scared," Lopa says, gripping her sister's shirt with both hands.

He says tomorrow again and hears himself lie.

Upstairs he packs again and sits on the edge of the bed with the eggs on the table while Pelangi Reach closes itself around the night: shutters, water pumps, someone laughing too hard by the pizza ovens, the robot dog clicking over tile below. Outside this place the world has teeth. Checkpoints. Lists. Men who ask second questions. Ports that remember faces. Here, people know his name in the morning.

The song comes back, not from outside but from under his ribs.

Upstairs he packs again and sits on the edge of the bed with the eggs on the table while Pelangi Reach closes itself around the night: shutters, water pumps, someone laughing too hard by the pizza ovens, the robot dog clicking over tile below. Outside this place the world has teeth. Checkpoints. Lists. Men who ask second questions. Ports that remember faces. Here, people know his name in the morning.

He takes the knife from the table and puts it in the bag.

---

Before the tide he goes up to the garden one last time. The tomatoes will die without him and will probably die anyway. His father would have known the thing they needed. Eli kneels, puts his warm hands in the damp soil, finds nothing in it that his father found, and leaves them where they are.

Suresh's boat is not one of the clean racing boats beyond the marina wall. It is low and broad and diesel-stained, already loaded with sacks of rice, two crates of limes, a cooler of fish, and a bundle wrapped so carefully in blue tarp that Eli knows better than to ask about it.

"If you come, you work," Suresh says.

Eli puts his bag under the forward bench and takes the stern line without being told. The tide pulls at the hull. He lets it pull, waits for the slack, then drops the loop over the cleat and makes it fast.

Suresh looks at the knot.

"Ugly," he says. "But it holds."

Eli steps aboard when Suresh tells him, not before. The deck shifts under him, a small working movement, nothing like the bright sailboats the Forest Foundry students race in the bay, all carbon spars and shouted counts and clean white wakes. This boat carries weight. It does not answer quickly. He bends his knees and keeps one hand free.

"Good," Suresh says. "Don't get proud."

They nose out before the sky changes. Pelangi Reach stands behind them in the dark, half-built and full of sleeping people. The waterparks are black shapes around their empty pools; the sailboats knock softly at their moorings; under the shade sails, the automatic chess boards move pieces through games no one is watching. In the plaza the robot dog crosses on its faithful little route — head down, head up, head down again — a machine pretending to patrol a home, or a home pretending the machine is a pet.

Eli looks away first.

Suresh brings the engine up, slow. "I am not taking you to the man."

"I know."

"No. You don't. I am taking rice and limes to Malacca. You are extra weight. At the pier you carry one crate and walk off when I say. You don't wait for me. You don't look back at me. You don't say my name in that city."

"All right."

"If a checkpoint asks, you helped load in Pelangi Reach. You want day work. You are stupid but useful."

"I can do that."

Suresh snorts. "Everyone can do that until someone asks the second question."

The strait opens and the resort lights thin behind them. Ahead, other boats move without showing much of themselves — fishers, ferries running half schedules, one police launch idling near a buoy with its blue light off. The war has made everyone economical with brightness.

Suresh cuts the engine back as the police launch turns near the buoy. For half a minute they move with barely a wake, the boat ticking under them.

"Movement remembers," he says. "A musician gets paid. A fuel man hears a name. Someone at the chandlery knows which rooms are watched." He brings the engine up one notch. "By morning, five people know too much and none of them know the same thing."

"Then why tell me any of it?"

"Because if I don't, you will ask the wrong person in Malacca and get Mei's kitchen burned down."

Eli holds the rail. The metal is wet.

Suresh keeps his eyes on the dark water. "I should have sent the band away."

"You didn't know."

"I knew enough to dislike it." He adjusts the throttle. "That's not the same as doing something."

For a while there is only engine noise and the slap of water under the bow. Eli shifts when the boat shifts; when Suresh tells him to move a crate forward, he moves it; when Suresh tells him to sit low as a patrol passes, he sits low. When they come alongside a quarantine float, Suresh tosses a packet of cigarettes into a plastic bucket on a pole without breaking speed, and a man in a mask looks into the bucket, looks at the boat, and finds something more interesting in the dark.

"That was a bribe," Eli says.

"That was hospitality."

"Looked like a bribe."

"Then don't look."

The sky goes gray. Malacca begins as cranes and roofs, then old walls, then the river mouth opening between buildings that seem to have survived by agreeing to be useful. A yellow quarantine banner hangs from the customs shed; two soldiers sit under it with rifles across their knees while a woman in a white vest points a thermometer at a fisherman's forehead and waves him through before it beeps.

Suresh cuts the engine to idle.

"One door," he says.

"The chandlery."

"Ask once."

"I know."

"No," Suresh says. "You remember. Knowing comes later."

He hands Eli a paper with the chandlery name written on it. No address. No number. Just the name, one word, and a line beneath it.

Ask once.

## Chapter Three — The Reopening

Malacca smells of rain on old stone and diesel on the river.

The supply boat puts him down before noon with a crate of limes in his arms and three men ahead of him who do not look at the checkpoint until the checkpoint looks away. The pier is open again, but not simply open — nothing is, now.

He folds the paper smaller and keeps it in his palm until the ink warms soft.

The city has learned to move around the wound. Stalls have reopened under tarps; tourists have not come back in numbers yet, but the signs wait for them anyway, bright and patient, promising river cruises and heritage walks and rooms with air-conditioning. A trishaw covered in plastic flowers goes by with no passenger in it, music playing to no one. Two men argue over a crate of mangoes as if mangoes are still the main problem in the world. Eli buys tea from a woman under a blue umbrella because he needs somewhere to stand.

"You looking for work?" she says.

"No."

"Then don't stand like that."

He moves half a step. She snorts and gives him the tea too hot to hold. He holds it anyway. She notices and decides not to.

The chandlery is off a lane that should be too narrow for a van and is not. Rope hangs in coils from the ceiling; nets, hooks, sealed batteries, cheap knives in blister packs, and tins of grease crowd the shelves the way ship's stores always crowd a room that has learned to hold too much. A boy of twelve or thirteen sits behind the counter doing sums in a schoolbook. He looks up once and goes back to the page.

Eli says the word from Suresh's paper.

The boy's pencil stops.

From the back room a man says, "We don't sell that."

Eli waits.

The man comes out wiping his hands on a rag. He is square and gray and has one eye clouded white. He looks at Eli's face, then his hands, then the street behind him.

"Wrong shop," he says.

Eli should leave. Suresh said one door. One door, and this is it. But the words have gone into his body and stopped there. Wrong shop. They do not feel like a refusal. They feel like a test someone forgot to explain.

"I came from Pelangi Reach," Eli says.

The man with the white eye looks at the boy. The boy closes his schoolbook.

"Everyone comes from somewhere."

"A man here asked after my mother."

"Men ask after women all day."

"English," Eli says. "Her name was Maren Das. Das was the name she used in her work."

The man's face does not change. That is how Eli knows it has landed.

Outside, a van passes the mouth of the lane. White. Clean. A blue medical seal on the door. It rolls slowly enough for Eli to see the driver not looking into the shop.

The man with the white eye watches the van in a small mirror nailed above the fishing line. His face gives nothing away.

"The man you came for was meant to meet you at the river steps after prayer," he says. "He moved this morning."

"Moved where?"

"Away from the river. He said something lit up on the steps."

The words find the same place in Eli the song found. Lit up.

"Where is he now?"

"He didn't want me giving that to the first person who came in asking after the music."

Eli puts both hands on the counter. The boy looks at them, and then away.

"He gave the band the song."

"Yes."

"He knew someone would know it."

"Yes."

"Then he wanted me here?"

"He wanted you alive when you got here." The man leans forward. His voice drops. "Those are not the same thing."

The van passes the lane again, going the other way.

The man takes a receipt pad from under the counter and writes an address so fast the numbers are almost nothing. He tears the paper off, folds it, and tucks it under a coil of thin green cord.

"You buy cord," he says.

Eli buys cord. The boy rings it up with hands that shake only once.

Outside, the day sits heavy in the lane, all wet stone, fryer oil, and engine heat. Eli walks without looking at the paper until he has turned twice and the shop is behind him. Then he opens it.

Old hotel. Fourth floor. Back stair.

Under that, in smaller writing:

If a woman tells you to run, run.

---

By late afternoon the rain comes hard and brief, the kind that hits the street like thrown gravel and then leaves steam rising from the drains.

Eli is two streets from the old hotel when he sees why the chandlery man warned him. Two health workers have set up a folding table near the bridge — one taking names, one watching screens — with a soldier beside them pretending not to be with them. Down by the river steps another white van waits with its rear doors closed.

They are not checking everyone. They are making a net around one approach.

The tingle in Eli's hands goes sharp, as if the skin has learned to listen. He crosses with a group of women carrying umbrellas and keeps the hotel in the corner of his eye.

At the next corner, someone catches his wrist.

He turns with the old movement. Thumb line. Elbow. Step in.

The woman lets go before he can take the joint.

"Don't," she says.

She is a little older than he is, maybe not by much. Short hair under a cap. Rainwater on her cheek. A courier bag across her chest. Nothing about her says police. Nothing about her says safe.

Behind her, at the far end of the covered way, one of the men from the health table looks up from his screen.

"The front of the hotel is watched," she says. "The river steps are worse."

"Who are you?"

"Someone trying to get another person out of the same building."

That is the first clear thing anyone in Malacca has said to him.

"You know the man I'm meeting?"

"No. I know the watchers." Her eyes go once to his hands. "They are not checking for fever. They are watching for activation. Hands, head, whatever lights up."

The charge in his palms gives one hard pulse.

She feels him feel it. He sees that she does. Her face closes.

The man from the table has started toward them. Not fast. Fast would warn everyone.

The woman takes a packet of cigarettes from her bag and puts it into Eli's hand. Inside there is no tobacco, only paper, stale mint, and something flat tucked under the foil.

"Laundry door on the back lane," she says. "The pass won't work. The plastic shim will. Fourth floor. If you keep moving, they follow you and I get four minutes for my extraction."

"And if I don't?"

"Then they follow me, and you walk into the front door like an idiot ten minutes later."

Then she smiles past him, bright and false, and lifts one hand.

"There you are," she says, loud enough for the man from the table. "You left your change."

Eli has spent enough of his life being nobody to know when someone has handed him a part.

"Sorry," he says.

He takes the packet. He does not look back until the corner.

When he does, she is already walking toward the health table, shoulders loose, face bored, giving them someone else to read and doing it well enough to make him angry.

The man turns with her.

Four minutes.

Eli runs.

---

The old hotel is red under the dirt, half its windows boarded and half hung with laundry lines. Someone has taped a reopening notice to the lobby glass and then given up on reopening. The keycard in the cigarette packet opens nothing; the strip of notched plastic beside it works on the laundry door because the lock is old and insulted by effort.

Inside smells of bleach and wet cloth.

He takes the stairs two at a time. On the second landing a child sits with a bowl of rice, watching cartoons on a cracked phone; the child looks at him, Eli puts a finger to his mouth, and the child considers this, then points up with the spoon.

"Terima kasih," Eli whispers.

"You say it funny," the child says, and goes back to the phone.

On the fourth floor the corridor is open to the river air. He can see the steps below and, now that he is above them, the box the chandlery man meant: the health table, the soldier, the van near the bridge, two men under the awning by the closed tour office. Not waiting for everyone. Waiting for one route and one shape.

His shape.

At the far end of the corridor, a door is open.

There is a man inside, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed. Thin. Soaked through. One hand pressed to his ribs. His hair is white at the temples and black everywhere else, as if fear started there and ran out of patience.

He is the witness. Eli knows it before the man speaks, because there is an oilcloth packet on the bed beside him, no larger than a hand. Someone has tied it with red thread gone almost black from handling.

The man looks up, and the relief on his face is so naked that Eli hates him for it.

"No," Eli says. "You brought them with you."

The man laughs once, or coughs. "You have her mouth."

"That was hers?"

"Listen to me."

"Was it hers?"

The man closes his eyes. "You followed the music. So did they."

The room goes small.

Eli looks at the oilcloth. The man sees him look and shakes his head before Eli can move.

"It is hers," the man says. "And if you open it here, you waste everything she did to keep it closed."

"What is it?"

"A record. Names, places, enough truth to get people killed." He swallows. "Enough truth to make you forget there are living people in the room."

Below, in the street, someone shouts.

The man reaches for Eli's wrist and misses. "Your mother said if this ever found you, you were to choose the living person over the record."

Eli stands very still.

The charge is no longer only in his hands. It is behind his eyes and under his skin, everywhere.

"What living person?"

"Me, if you can." The man looks toward the stairs. "Anyone, if you can't. Not the packet. Not first."

"Who are you?"

"A coward," the man says. "Later, if there is later, someone can give you the nicer version."

Footsteps hit the stairs below. More than one person. Fast now.

The man looks toward the door, then back to Eli.

"Roof— hatch at the end— forget the packet—"

The oilcloth on the bed pulls at him the way the song did. Names. Places. Her work in his hands if he just reaches.

Eli reaches for the man's arm instead.

"Up," he says.

The man stares at him like he did not think anyone would do that.

"She told you—"

"I heard you." Eli gets under his shoulder. The man is lighter than he looks and smells of blood and river rain. "Not first. Move."

They make the hall as the door below hits the wall.

The hatch sticks once. Rain, river air, the health table and the white van far below like toys. Eli boosts the man through, follows, pulls the hatch shut on the landing noise.

The roofs here lean close. The man cannot run. Eli half-carries him over the first gap, the second—

His hands flare — the same light that brought them to the steps — and on the far roof the woman in the white vest looks up from her thermometer table.

"Down," the man says. "Not up. They'll box the roof—"

A fire escape drops toward the back lane. Eli goes first to clear it, then comes back for the man.

Footsteps on the tile behind them. Not one person. The rhythm is trained.

At the foot of the ladder the back lane is empty for three heartbeats.

Then engines — two scooters, white coats, no fever checks — turning into the lane from both ends.

The man shoves Eli toward the gap between them.

"Go—"

"I didn't come for the packet," Eli says, which is not the same as knowing what he came for, and then he is moving anyway because the first rider is off the scooter and closing fast and the man's ribs are not going to survive what comes next.

Eli puts himself between them.

The first rider is faster than he looks. A handlebars swing, a forearm to the throat, and Eli is back on the wet brick before he has processed the impact. The second man has a short stick. The third has a knife that looks like equipment, not improvisation. They are not trying to arrest him. They are trying to clear the lane.

He reads the line. He has done this enough times in the Pelangi Reach training mornings — angle, range, entry, follow — and his body knows the shape of it. What his body does not know is that three men who move like this are not going to leave a witness, and the lane is too narrow for the Kali to work the way it was meant to.

The first stick catches his wrist. The second catches his ribs. He turns the knife with his forearm and takes a shallow cut — heat, then cold — and then the first rider is on him, hands around his throat, and the world tilts because pressure at the neck reaches the old fracture faster than anything else.

He does not feel the brick under his heels. He feels the water first — rain in the lane, the blacker water at the gap by the chandlery — and then a hard shove between the shoulder blades.

He goes over.

The back of his head does not hit the water first. The first thing is the lack of air. The second is the cold finding the cut in his forearm as if it has been waiting. He is strong enough to kick, strong enough to reach, but the hands on his throat are not the hands of someone who is going to get tired. The water at the chandlery gap is shallow and smells of diesel and rot. It is not deep enough to drown in. It is deep enough to hold him under.

He thinks he hears the chandlery man's door. He thinks he hears the boy's schoolbook slap shut. The hands do not loosen.

The old fracture hums. Names blur into names he does not know. His mother's hum goes empty and then comes back as a woman's laugh he has never heard. The river under him seems to breathe. The roof far above tilts. A fire-escape ladder rings once, metal on stone.

The first thing he sees on the other side is light.

He is not above the lane. He is below it, in the water, looking up through diesel sheen, and the light is under his skin. His hands. The same warmth he carries on the line, the same thing that never burns him, has come up his arms and settled behind his eyes and in the base of his skull and it is not warm anymore — it is sharp and loud and it makes him see.

The surface breaks open.

The woman from the health table is leaning over the chandlery wall, thermometer hand frozen midair. Behind her, a second man has the first rider by the collar and is not looking at Eli at all. The third attacker is on his knees, hands over his ears, saying something Eli cannot hear over the noise in his own head.

He does not know that the light has gone out into the street and up into the grid.

He does not know that five directions have just lit up at once.

He only knows that the hands on his throat are gone, that his lungs are burning, that the brick wall under his back is cold, and that for the first time in his life he can feel the space around him as if it were another limb.

And then the field opens.

He feels the network for the first time — not his body, not a body at all, but a chord held across distance by stones he cannot see. Anywhere that is old, anywhere that was made to hold a charge, reaches back. The light he shed into the street draws it to him like a bell drawing sound.

He cannot tell where his skin ends.

A kitchen he has never walked into hums with the same hunger as the one under his hands. Rain on a roof becomes rain in a room that doesn't exist for him. A fire-escape ladder rings once, metal on stone, and it rings somewhere else as well — in a tower he cannot name, on a coast he has never seen, held by a conductor buried before his language existed. The cognition is not his. The world is.

Each contact is a door, but the rooms behind them are not people. They are moments held in stone: a grief that was pressed into a layout when the layout was new, a breath taught to a circle before the circle was broken, a hand laid on a pillar and left there. They do not know him. They only know that something alive has touched the wire.

A woman walking into a room with her hands already cold. A soldier writing a name in a book that is already wet. The rain on a roof. A wok over fire none of them are tending anymore.

He tries to stand and his legs are not his. He tries to breathe and the air coming into him is not all Malacca air — some of it is old, charged, caught in a throat that is not his throat. He tries to speak and the soldier's name comes out of a mouth that isn't his.

And then he is back. The lane is just a lane. The roof is just a roof. The light is leaving him, bleeding out into the grid. He cannot tell how much of him it took with it.

Through the noise, a sound he has never heard: boots on wet tile, coming down the lane from both ends. The Custodians have regrouped. The second rider is on his feet. The knife-man is wiping his blade on his jacket.

The chandlery door slams open. The boy with the schoolbook steps into the lane with a fire extinguisher in both hands and sprays the first rider in the face. The white eye man comes out behind him with a boat hook and drives the second man back against the wall. For twelve seconds there is a fight in the lane that is not about Eli.

Eli tries to stand and his legs are not his. He tries to speak and the soldier's name comes out of someone else's mouth. He tries to breathe and the girl's held air is not enough.

The chandlery door slams open.

The boy with the schoolbook steps into the lane with a fire extinguisher in both hands and sprays the first rider in the face. The white eye man comes out behind him with a boat hook and drives the second man back against the wall. For twelve seconds there is a fight in the lane that is not about Eli.

Then the chandlery man looks down at him and says something he will never forget.

"Not the packet," the man says. "Her son. That's who they're after."

The boy pulls Eli up by one arm. The white eye man takes the knife-man's wrist and breaks it the way a man breaks something he has broken before. Outside the lane, Malacca keeps happening: a moped coughs, someone sings, a shutters rattles.

The custodian team has seen the flare. They have seen the sevenfold signature pour through a body that has no business carrying it. They are not here for him anymore. They are here for whatever he has woken up.

Eli's legs give. The boy and the one-eyed man drag him inside, up the stairs, through the back. They put him on the floor of a room that smells of rope and schoolbooks and the river. The man shoves the oilcloth packet into his chest.

"You remind her of a door," the man says. "That's why they used the song. A door, not a target. You open it, they walk through."

He means Eli himself. He means the surge. He means the seven rooms that are now standing open inside him.

Eli cannot answer. His mouth fills with copper and the girl's held breath.

The footsteps run past the shop and keep going. For half a minute there is only the sound of the river and the boy breathing hard beside him.

Then footsteps come back. Slower. Separated.

The man with the white eye kneels beside him. His voice is the same as it was hours ago, when he told Eli to buy cord.

"Can you walk?"

Eli tries. It works, almost.

"Outside the back door. There's a lane the white vans don't watch. They will watch it in five minutes. You need to be gone before then."

The boy hands him the knife from the counter. It is one of the cheap blister-pack knives. The blade is thin. It will hold.

"How many?" Eli says.

"Two behind you. At least two ahead. The river path is bad. The lane is worse." The man looks at his own hands, then at Eli's, which are still glowing faintly. "I've seen what that does to a man's head. It doesn't stop."

"Then help me stop it."

The boy opens the door. The rain has broken into something fine and cold that finds every cut. The lane behind the shop is narrow and lined with stacked sacks of rice and nets. The river is a breath of air at the end of it. To the left, the van shapes sit at the mouth of the main street, movement inside.

Eli takes the wall. His left leg is slow. The girl's held air is still caught in his chest. The soldier's name is on his tongue and it is not his name.

He makes it to the mouth of the lane before the first van lights up.

A woman steps out from under the awning of a closed fish stall and takes his arm before he can brace. Her face is the same face that taught him the four-minute window, but she is not wearing the same expression. She is not bored. She is not performing.

"You're loud," she says. She does not mean the noise. She means the light, the seven rooms, the signature pouring off him like heat. "I felt you a block away."

She guides him left, away from the van, into a side corridor that smells of frying oil and river rot. A scooter engine flickers to life somewhere ahead.

"Where are we going?" Eli says.

"Not the chandlery. Not the river." She checks the corner, pulls him through. "I lost my extraction when you lit up. So now we're both running."

The chandlery man's instruction was clear. The boy's knife is in his hand. The seven rooms inside him are still open and each one has different weather.

But the woman beside him is not letting go of his arm.

"Can you keep up?" she says.

He answers by moving forward.
